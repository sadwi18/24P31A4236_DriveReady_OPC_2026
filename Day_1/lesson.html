The case study for the whole workshop

Sweet Crumbs Home Bakery takes cake orders on chat, writes them in a notebook and answers the same questions all day. Over the workshop we build it an AI team: a Marketing agent, a Sales & Support agent and an Operations agent. Today we lay the first brick: one API call that answers a bakery question.

01Why is AI suddenly everywhere?

AI is 70 years old. What is new is that anyone can use a powerful model with a few lines of code.

For decades, building AI meant collecting data, hiring PhDs and training your own model for months. Two shifts changed that. First, one general model now does a thousand jobs: the same model writes, summarises, translates and explains code. Second, the barrier collapsed: AI companies run the giant models on their servers and rent them out through an API (your code sends a request, their server sends an answer). Apps built this way are called wrappers.

Real world

The "Ask" button in a shopping app, a college fee-enquiry bot, a resume checker on a job site: most of these are small teams calling a model through an API, not companies that trained their own AI. At Sweet Crumbs, the owner will never train a model; we will rent one for a few paise a day.

Analogy

Swiggy instead of running a restaurant. Earlier, to sell biryani you needed your own kitchen. Now you order through Swiggy: the AI companies run the "kitchen" (the model), you place the order (the API call) and build your own brand on top, like a cloud kitchen.

02What an LLM really does

An LLM does one thing: it predicts the next piece of text, again and again, in a loop.

LLM = Large Language Model. Give it some text and it scores every possible next piece (token), picks one, adds it to the text and repeats until it decides to stop. Chatting, coding, summarising and translating are all built on this single skill.

Real world

Your phone keyboard suggesting the next word on WhatsApp, and Gmail finishing your sentence in grey, are small versions of the same idea. When ChatGPT's reply appears word by word, you are watching the loop run.

Analogy

Super-autocomplete, or a game of antakshari: each new word must fit naturally after the ones before it, played thousands of times per answer.

03Tokens: the unit of everything

A model reads and writes tokens, pieces of words. You pay per token and every limit is counted in tokens.

Common words are one token ("the", "cake"); rare words are split into pieces ("unbelievableness" → "un" + "believ" + "able" + "ness"). Rule of thumb: 1 token ≈ ¾ of an English word, so 300 words ≈ 400 tokens. Sub-word pieces keep the vocabulary to a fixed size (about 100,000 pieces) yet can spell any word, even one invented tomorrow.

Real world

AI providers price per million tokens, separately for input and output. A Telugu or Hindi chatbot needs more tokens than an English one for the same message, so it costs more to run. When we price the Sweet Crumbs bot, tokens are the number we multiply.

Analogy

Lego bricks. You don't need a special brick for every toy; a standard set builds anything. Tokens are the Lego bricks of language.

04Temperature: the creativity dial

Temperature decides how adventurous the model is when it picks the next token.

The model gives every possible next token a probability. Low temperature (0–0.3) almost always picks the top choice: focused and repeatable. High temperature (around 1.0) spreads the chance out: varied and creative. That is why the same question can get different answers.

Probabilities come from fixed example scores passed through softmax with your temperature: p = e^(score/T) ÷ Σ e^(score/T). Low T sharpens the top choice; high T flattens the spread.

Real world

A bank's support bot or a fee-enquiry bot runs at low temperature: the answer must be the same and correct every time. An Instagram caption generator runs high so every caption is different. At Sweet Crumbs, prices and orders use 0.2; Marketing's festival posts can use 1.0.

Analogy

A strict exam evaluator (low) accepts only the textbook answer. A creative-writing teacher (high) welcomes surprising answers.

05The model has no memory

Between calls, an LLM forgets everything. Chat apps only look like they remember because the app resends the whole conversation every time.

Each API call is independent. If you say "My name is Ravi" in one call and ask "What is my name?" in the next, the model has no idea. ChatGPT seems to remember because the ChatGPT app keeps your conversation and sends all of it with every new message. In your own chatbot, your code must do this.

06The context window: the size of the desk

The context window is the most tokens one call can hold: instructions, history, documents and the reply, together.

Every model has a fixed limit, for example 128,000 tokens. Everything you send and the answer must fit inside it. Fill it too much and the request is rejected or the reply is cut short. A fuller window is also slower and costs more, because you pay for every input token on every call.

Real world

Students paste a whole 300-page PDF, or 50 job descriptions at once, and the tool fails or answers badly. The fix engineers use: send one document at a time, keep only the relevant part, and summarise old chat. Sweet Crumbs' menu and rules are short, so they fit easily.

Analogy

A 5-litre bottle: you can't pour in more than 5 litres, and the bottle must also hold the reply. Fill it to the top with input and there's no room left for the answer.

07How it learned, and why it sometimes lies confidently

The model learned to write text that sounds right, not to check what is true. That gap is called hallucination.

Self-supervision: take any sentence from the internet, hide a word and ask the model to guess it. The original sentence is the answer key, so no human labelling is needed. Doing this billions of times, the model picks up grammar, facts and common sense as a side-effect. Try being the model:

Be the model: fill in the blankGame
The barista handed me a hot cup of ______.
Pick the word you think fills the blank. The original sentence on the internet is the answer key.

The catch: it learned what is plausible, not what is true. Ask about something it has never seen, such as your bakery's delivery policy, and it may confidently invent an answer. The fix is to give it the facts at question time and tell it to answer only from them (this is called RAG, and it is not retraining).

Real world

In 2024, a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to compensate a customer after the airline's website chatbot told him he could claim a bereavement discount after travelling. The airline's actual policy said he couldn't. The company was held responsible for its chatbot's wrong answer. For Sweet Crumbs, a made-up "same-day delivery" promise would be the same kind of mistake.

Analogy

A student in a viva who bluffs smoothly instead of saying "I don't know". RAG turns the viva into an open-book exam: the answer is on the page in front of them.

08The AI engineer: where you fit

An AI engineer builds real products on top of ready-made models, without training those models.

Someone else builds the model; you build the product around it. Your daily work: pick the model (good, fast and cheap enough), write the prompts, give it the right context, control cost, make it reliable and ship it as a web app, Android app or Telegram bot.

Every AI product, from a coding assistant to a bakery order bot, follows the same four steps:

Real world

Sweet Crumbs in the four steps: connect to a model on Groq · give context: menu, prices, rules · get the answer: "Yes, all our cakes come eggless" · wrap it in a Telegram bot. The same steps build a tuition-centre bot or a resume matcher; only the context and the interface change.

Analogy

A film director doesn't build cameras. They pick actors (models), write the script (prompts), set the scene (context), keep the budget (tokens) and deliver the film (the product).

09Your first API call, line by line

Four steps: bring the tools → unlock your key → set up the client → ask and print.

Step 0 · Put your key in Colab Secrets

  1. Get a free key at console.groq.com → API Keys. Your key is your password and your bill: never share it.
  2. In Colab, click the key icon in the left bar → Add new secret → name GROQ_API_KEY, paste the key, switch on Notebook access.
  3. Never paste the key into the code. A key on GitHub is found by bots within minutes.

The program

!pip install -q openai                           # once per Colab session

from google.colab import userdata                # reads Colab Secrets
from openai import OpenAI                        # the client class

client = OpenAI(
    api_key=userdata.get("GROQ_API_KEY"),        # who you are, who pays
    base_url="https://api.groq.com/openai/v1",   # send requests to Groq, not OpenAI
)

response = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="openai/gpt-oss-20b",                  # which model answers
    messages=[
        {"role": "system", "content": "You are a funny joker"},
        {"role": "user",   "content": "tell me a joke"},
    ],
)
print(response.choices[0].message.content)       # the reply text

Design lens (for those who know OOP)

openai is the package, OpenAI the class (a blueprint), client the object. The client is a Facade over HTTP, JSON, authentication and retries; chat and completions are nested objects (composition); the key is passed in by constructor injection, read from configuration outside the code.

Today's build: Sweet Crumbs' first answer

Same five lines, now with the bakery's facts in the system message and a low temperature, using everything from today.

SYSTEM = """ROLE:   You are the assistant for Sweet Crumbs Home Bakery.
FACTS:  We bake to order. Orders need 24 hours' notice. All cakes have eggless versions.
        Plum cake Rs 500. Chocolate cake Rs 650. Cupcake Rs 80.
RULES:  Never promise a delivery time. If the answer isn't in FACTS, say "I'll check with the owner."
FORMAT: Under 50 words, friendly, English or Telugu matching the customer."""

response = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="openai/gpt-oss-20b",
    temperature=0.2,                              # facts and prices: keep it focused
    messages=[{"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM},
              {"role": "user",   "content": "Can I get an eggless cake today evening?"}],
)
print(response.choices[0].message.content)
print(response.usage)                             # tokens used = what you pay for
Expected behaviour

The bot should say eggless is available but orders need 24 hours' notice, so today evening isn't possible. If it promises "yes, today", read the system message again: which fact did it ignore?

Your 10%: try it different ways

  1. Temperature: ask "Write a tagline for Sweet Crumbs" three times at 0.2, then three times at 1.2. Compare.
  2. Hallucination: delete the FACTS line and ask "Do you deliver to Kakinada?" What does it invent?
  3. No memory: make two separate calls: "My name is Ravi", then "What is my name?". Then put both in one messages list and try again.
  4. Tokens: ask the same question in English and in Telugu and compare response.usage.prompt_tokens.
  5. Your business: rewrite SYSTEM for the business you chose (5 facts, 3 rules) and ask 3 real customer questions.
